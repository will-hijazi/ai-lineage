AI Lineage v1.5
by Will Hijazi

The AI universe, as a family tree

    Era 1 of 9

    Roots

    before 1956

    AI did not start with computers. It grew out of six older fields, one at the top of each column: logic (reasoning with rules), optimization (finding the best choice), statistics (learning from data), neuroscience (how the brain works), information theory (measuring and predicting messages) and control theory (how a system corrects itself). In 1943 a neuron was first described as a simple on-off switch, and in 1947 the simplex method was invented to plan for the US Air Force. Both ideas are still at work today.

    Era 2 of 9

    The birth of AI

    1956 to 1979

    A summer workshop at Dartmouth in 1956 gave the field its name. Early AI bet on rules and search: write down how an expert reasons, then let the computer explore the options. At the same time the perceptron showed that a machine could learn its own weights from its mistakes. Then came the first trap. A 1969 book showed that a one-layer perceptron cannot learn even a simple either-or pattern (XOR), promises ran ahead of results, and funding dried up: the first AI winter.

    Era 3 of 9

    Learning from data

    1980 to 2008

    Hand-written rules proved too brittle, and a second winter arrived in the late 1980s. The field turned to learning from data instead. Backpropagation (1986) trained networks with hidden layers, and convolutional networks learned to read handwritten digits. Statistics produced the workhorses you still use on tables: decision trees, random forests, support vector machines and gradient boosting. Reinforcement learning took shape: learn by trial, error and reward. Meanwhile the web began producing data at a scale nobody had seen.

    Era 4 of 9

    Deep learning

    2009 to 2016

    Everything changed in 2012. AlexNet, a deep convolutional network trained on GPUs with the ImageNet photographs, won the image recognition contest by a wide margin. Deep networks took over vision, then words: word embeddings turned each word into a list of numbers, and sequence-to-sequence models with attention translated sentences. Open-source tools put all of it in everyone's hands. In 2016 AlphaGo joined deep networks, reinforcement learning and tree search to beat a Go champion: three columns meeting in one model.

    Era 5 of 9

    Attention and scale

    2017 to 2021

    In 2017 the transformer dropped step-by-step reading and took in a whole passage at once, using attention to weigh every word against every other. Pre-trained on vast amounts of web text, transformers became language models: BERT to understand text, GPT to write it. Scaling laws showed that bigger models trained on more data improve predictably, and GPT-3 (2020) could do a new task from a few examples. The same attention idea spread to images, to images paired with words (CLIP), and to image generation.

    Era 6 of 9

    Assistants

    2022 to 2024

    A language model only predicts the next word. To become an assistant it was fine-tuned on examples of good answers, then shaped by human feedback: people ranked replies, and reinforcement learning, crossing over from the right-hand column, taught the model to prefer the better ones. ChatGPT launched in November 2022, followed by Claude and Gemini in 2023. Retrieval let assistants look facts up before answering, and reasoning models learned to work through a problem before replying. The trap: a fluent answer can be confidently wrong.

    Era 7 of 9

    Agents

    2022 to 2026

    An agent is a language model placed in a loop: it plans, calls a tool, reads the result, and decides the next step until the job is done. Follow the column down. In 2022 the ReAct paper showed a model could alternate reasoning and actions. In 2023 AutoGPT and BabyAGI wired a model into a loop that set its own steps: exciting, but it often went in circles. Function calling (2023) gave models reliable tools, computer use (2024) let them see a screen and click, and the Model Context Protocol (2024) gave every tool a common plug. In 2025 agents became products: coding agents such as Claude Code and Codex write and test software, research agents read and report, and ChatGPT agent took over web tasks. In 2026 the same loop reached everyday office work: Claude Cowork and Microsoft Copilot Cowork run multi-step jobs across files and apps, and OpenClaw made a self-hosted personal agent one of the fastest-growing projects on GitHub. The traps are new too: hidden instructions can hijack an agent, and an agent can do more than you asked.

    Era 8 of 9

    Defense

    today

    Three columns above also reach the military. Computer vision: Project Maven began in 2017 by analysing drone video, and Palantir's Maven Smart System now fuses satellite, drone and signals data into one picture; the Pentagon ordered it made a formal program of record in 2026, and NATO bought its own version in 2025. Reinforcement learning and teaming: an AI flew the X-62A test jet in dogfights in 2023, and the Air Force's Collaborative Combat Aircraft are uncrewed wingmen that fly alongside crewed fighters. Assistants: since December 2025 GenAI.mil has given defense staff Gemini, then Grok and ChatGPT, for sensitive but unclassified work, and about 1.7 million people use it. The traps travel too: a confident wrong answer or a hijacked agent costs more here, and how much a human stays in control of lethal decisions is the central policy question.

    Era 9 of 9

    Where it landed

    today

    Follow any column down and it ends in real work. Classical machine learning still runs most prediction on tables: maintenance, delays, fraud. Optimization still plans routes, crews and airlift. Deep learning reads medical scans; transformers help predict protein structures and transcribe speech; generative models make images; agents write software. The newest thing rarely replaces the oldest, and choosing the right tool for the question is still the real skill.
